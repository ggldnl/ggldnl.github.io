Differential Drive Robot

Mobile robotics Control systems ROS

The completed differential drive robot

Project Overview

Being a robotics engineering student, I found myself taking a course in Autonomous and Mobile Robotics, which dealt with `planning and control methods for achieving mobility and autonomy in mobile robots`. I've always been a practical learner and I knew I had to build a robot to really get into the nuts and bolts and to make sense of what I was hearing in class.

I decided to go for the simplest mobile robot I could think of that is still of practical utility: a differential drive robot. It follows the same kinematic model as a unicycle, which is the simplest model that captures meaningful mobility, but it doesn't need additional control to stay upright.

Some other aspects I wanted to explore were:

  • I wanted it to be a template for future mobile robots — something modular and easy to upgrade in all of its parts. What if I want to swap the motors for something more powerful, try different encoders or even use a different chassis? I designed and built it so that every component could be swapped or upgraded without having to start over and I kept this principle in mind even when writing the software.
  • I remember another professor, in a different course, mentioning the fact that good robot design often involves two levels of control: a high-level running heavy but slow stuff — planning, algorithms, communication — and a low-level stage dealing with motor control, sensor reading and everything that needs to happen in near-real time. I wanted to explore this architecture in practice, so my robot uses a Raspberry Pi for the high-level logic (running ROS nodes, handling communication) and an Arduino for the low-level tasks (keeping the wheels spinning at the right speed, reading encoders, running the control loop).
  • I wanted to experiment the low-level motor control itself. Two motors from the same manufacturer and even from the same batch can behave slightly differently — one may spin faster than the other under the same voltage. Without compensation, that can ruin the trajectory of the robot. The solution is to add a feedback controller and I chose to implement a PID controller so that, regardless of small hardware differences, the robot could move accurately.

This post provides an overview of the project, showing the design process from start to finish.

Hardware

The circuit is very simple.

Circuit diagram

I should've used some diodes to prevent current from flowing backwards into the power supply. This happens when, for example, the arduino is being programmed. The UBEC should include some kind of protection but I'm not sure about that, I found very little information about it.
  • An Arduino Nano handles the low level stuff (controls the motors, reads the encoders and sensors, ...).
  • A raspberry pi zero orchestrates everything. It will receive messages from other ROS nodes and command the Arduino to do something.
  • The little black square is a board featuring a DRV8833 h-bridge. An h-bridge allows a DC motor to be driven in both forward and reverse directions by switching the polarity of the voltage applied to the motor. The DRV8833 in particular supports variable speed both formward and backwards when a PWM signal is used.
  • The motors I chose are N20 DC motors. They are rated for 60RPM at 6V. They have a two-phase magnetic encoder each that allows you to compute both the direction and the velocity in which the motor is spinning.
  • The two resistors are necessary to make the Arduino and Raspberry talk to each other. The UART is a common serial communication protocol that uses two pins, RX (receive data pin) and TX (transmit data pin), along with VCC and GND. The GND is shared but we need to pay attention to the VCC. The Arduino uses 5V logic so we'll need the two resistors to implement a voltage divider that will bring the voltage on its pins down to 3.3V.
  • In the schematics you can notice I reserved two 4-pin slots, one with the 5V, GND, SDA, SCL pins and another one with pins from A0 to A3. This way we can interface with I2C devices (SDA, SCL) and, with pins A0 to A3, with a shift register. I specifically thought about this because one of the things I wanted to experiment with was integrating ToF sensors. The sensors I had work with I2C but the problem is that each of them has the same I2C address. In order to use more than one of them we need to, in turn, enable it (XShut pin), change address and disable it. A shift register would have been just what we needed.

Other components are:

  • The Power Supply Unit, the sketchiest of the parts I've used since I did not find much about it. It's a ZMR 8A Dual BEC I believe, I ordered it from aliexpress for a few bucks. I took it because it features two independent outputs with a voltage selector. This way we can power the motors at 6V and the other electronics at 5V. I had to remove the top cover and the outer insulation film and to replace the connector.
  • The battery, a 2P 500mAh Lipo. These kind of batteries are usually found in RC planes and cars.

After prototyping I decided to design a PCB and mount everything on it. This way, I didn't have to deal with messy cables all around and I was able to reduce everything to a disc with a 10 cm diameter.

PCB

Manufactured PCB

Note that the manufactured PCB in the image comes from a previous design. Nothing has changed with the electronics but I added a few mounting holes and the caster wheel to the silkscreen.

I printed the supports in PLA (Matte black from Bambulab) and the wheels in TPU to add extra grippyness even though it wasn't as much as I hoped for. I used M2 heat set inserts and screws to mount everything. The wheels are just press fitted and I used an M3 washer on each to separate them from the motor. Mounting the batteries is always tricky. I often use Velcro to hold them in place. The assembly is overall so trivial that I won't get too much into details.

Another aspect worth mentioning is the caster wheel. I used a simple metal ball that I had lying around (who remembers Geomag?) with a 10mm diameter and I printed a mount that allows it to rotate more or less freely.
